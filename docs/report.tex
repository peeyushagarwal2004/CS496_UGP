\begin{abstract}
\noindent The adoption of microscaling formats in deep neural network (DNN) accelerators
reduces the cost of low-precision inference, but their impact on DNN reliability under
hardware faults remains largely unexplored. Unlike conventional floating-point
representations, microscaling formats represent multiple values using a shared scale, making
the effect of a bit fault dependent not only on the corrupted value but also on the scale and
other values within the scaling block. We propose a reliability analysis methodology for
microscaling-based DNNs that characterizes how faults in element values and shared scaling
factors propagate through the network. The methodology evaluates fault sensitivity across
different microscaling formats, block sizes, tensor types, and fault locations, and
identifies the representation characteristics that make a model more vulnerable to faults.
We conduct an extensive fault-injection study on representative CNN and Transformer models
using weight and activation tensors. Our results show that reliability varies substantially
across microscaling formats even when they provide comparable inference accuracy, and that
faults affecting shared scaling information can have a significantly different impact from
faults in individual elements.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Keywords:} DNN reliability, microscaling formats, MXFP, block
floating-point, fault injection, shared scale, fault sensitivity, low-precision inference.
\end{abstract}

I have chosen to tell it in order, rather than only presenting the final tables, for one

failed taught me as much as the results that held. Where a result was later revised, I say
so at the point it first appears, and Section~\ref{sec:revised} collects all the revisions in
one place.

\paragraph{The results in brief.} The core hypothesis holds on every network I tested.
\begin{itemize}
\item MX formats with the same accuracy differ in fault vulnerability by up to three orders
of magnitude. Measured per inference, the ordering e3m2 $<$ e4m3 $<$ e5m2 holds on all eight
CIFAR-10 configurations and all nine CIFAR-100 networks tested.
\item The main cause is the NaN and infinity codes of the 8-bit formats. A fault that turns
an element into NaN corrupts every inference, and on the larger models such faults carry
97--100\% of all element damage. Decoding those codes as zero when they are read cuts
element damage by at least $29\times$ on those models.
\item A fault in the shared scale is always more damaging than a fault in an element,
typically by $13\times$ to more than $1000\times$.
\item The conventional ``any image changed'' SDC metric can hide real differences between
formats. I replaced it with a per-inference rate, which is stable across evaluation sets.
\item Both open design choices in the brief are settled: single-bit injection is sufficient,
and exact enumeration over the MX code table beats learned value intervals by two orders of
magnitude.
\end{itemize}

\section{The project brief}
\label{sec:brief}

The project started from a working draft titled \emph{Reliability-Aware Fault Injection for
Microscaling-Based DNNs: Problem Statement, Fault Model \& Study Design}, dated August 2026.
Its text is reproduced below without changes, apart from typesetting.

\subsection{Motivation and problem statement}

Microscaling (MX) formats cut the cost of low-precision inference by having a block of $K$
elements share one scaling factor: the stored value is $v_{i,s} = m_{i,s}\cdot
2^{(X_s-\mathrm{bias})}$, where $m_{i,s}$ is a narrow 4--8-bit element and $X_s$ is an 8-bit
scale (E8M0) shared across the block. This coupling changes the fault model fundamentally. In
conventional floating-point every value is a self-contained word, so a bit fault perturbs
exactly one value. Under microscaling a fault's effect depends on the corrupted value, on the
shared scale, and on the other values in the block --- and it can occur at two structurally
different sites:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{p{0.44\linewidth}|p{0.44\linewidth}}
\textsf{\textbf{SITE r --- ELEMENT}} & \textsf{\textbf{SITE X --- SHARED SCALE}} \\
\textbf{Per-element value.} A narrow 4/3/2-bit element $m$. A fault corrupts a single value. &
\textbf{Per-block shared scale.} One 8-bit scale shared by $K$ elements. A fault rescales the whole block. \\
\texttt{blast radius $\rightarrow$ 1 value} & \texttt{blast radius $\rightarrow$ K values ($\times2^{\Delta X}$)} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Despite the rapid adoption of microscaling in DNN accelerators, its reliability under
transient hardware faults is largely unexplored. This project provides a reliability-analysis
methodology that characterizes how element and shared-scale faults propagate, and identifies
which \textbf{representation characteristics} --- format, block size, tensor type, and fault
location --- make a model more or less vulnerable.

\briefbox{CORE HYPOTHESIS}{Reliability of microscaling DNNs varies substantially across
formats \textbf{even at comparable inference accuracy}, and faults in the \textbf{shared
scaling factor} behave very differently from faults in individual elements --- because a
single scale fault rescales an entire block of $K$ values at once.}

\subsection{Objectives}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{O\arabic*}]
\item \textbf{Fault propagation.} Characterize how single-bit faults in element values vs.\
shared scaling factors propagate through the network, and quantify the difference in impact.
\item \textbf{Format sensitivity.} Measure fault sensitivity across microscaling formats
(MXFP8/6/4 and element variants) and test whether reliability differs even at comparable
accuracy.
\item \textbf{Block-size effect.} Determine how block size (scale-sharing granularity) trades
inference efficiency against reliability.
\item \textbf{Tensor \& location.} Compare sensitivity across tensor types (weights vs.\
activations) and fault locations (scale bit vs.\ element bit; MSB vs.\ LSB; per layer).
\item \textbf{Vulnerability drivers.} Identify the representation characteristics (dynamic
range, exponent/mantissa split, scale granularity) that explain observed vulnerability.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Fault model}

We adopt the single-bit-flip model standard in DNN fault-injection studies, generalized to
the two microscaling sites, for both weight and activation tensors. Fault
\textbf{occurrence} (where/how often a bit flips) is modeled separately from fault
\textbf{impact} (how much the decoded value changes).

\paragraph{Element vs.\ shared-scale faults.}
\begin{align*}
&\text{element flip (bit $b$ of $m$):} && d = |\log v' - \log v| && \text{--- 1 value}\\
&\text{scale flip (bit $b$ of $X$):} && v'_{i,s} = m_{i,s}\cdot 2^{(X'-\mathrm{bias})}\ \ \forall i && \text{--- all $K$ values}\\
&\text{block severity:} && D_s = \textstyle\sum_i |\log v' - \log v| = K\cdot|X'-X|\cdot\log 2
\end{align*}
\emph{A single shared-scale fault rescales the entire block by 2 raised to $(X'-X)$, giving
it a blast radius of $K$ --- the structural reason scale faults differ from element faults.}

\paragraph{Occurrence: per-position rate and multiplicity.}
\[
\pi_{\mathrm{site},b} = \pi^{0}_{\mathrm{site}}\cdot\kappa_{\mathrm{site}}(b),\qquad
P(S) = \prod_{b\in S}\pi_b\cdot\prod_{b\notin S}(1-\pi_b),\qquad
H_{\mathrm{site}} = 1-\prod_b(1-\pi_b) \approx \sum_b\pi_b + \sum_{b<b'}\pi_b\pi_{b'} + O(\pi^3)
\]
\emph{$\kappa(b)$ = per-bit-position susceptibility profile (mean 1); a graded profile
reproduces higher rates on more significant bits, $\kappa\equiv1$ gives the uniform
single-bit model. $P(S)$ = Poisson--binomial pattern probability; $H$ = word-corruption
rate, whose single-bit term is the baseline and multi-bit tail an optional extension.}

\subsection{Methodology}

\textbf{Quantization.} Quantize model weights and activations to each microscaling
configuration; confirm baseline accuracy so reliability is compared at matched accuracy
where possible.

\noindent\textbf{Injection engine.} Bit-addressable MXFP representation enabling faults at a chosen
bit of either the shared scale or an element, for weights (static) and activations (per
input, via hooks); golden-vs-faulty comparison detects top-1 changes and silent data
corruption.

\noindent\textbf{Campaign.} For each cell of the study matrix, run a statistical fault-injection
campaign at a target confidence/error. Value-aware stratified sampling (in the spirit of
TreeFI, ICCAD 2026) is used as a supporting tool to keep the large multi-axis campaign
tractable; it is not the contribution.

\noindent\textbf{Ground truth.} Exhaustive fault injection on a small model (ResNet8) where the
microscaling bit space is small enough, providing a reference to validate the statistical
estimates.

\noindent\textbf{Analysis.} Compare fault sensitivity across the four axes; contrast shared-scale vs.\
element faults; and correlate vulnerability with representation characteristics to explain
the trends.

\briefbox{SUPPORTING TOOL, NOT THE CONTRIBUTION}{TreeFI-style value-aware stratified sampling
is referenced only to make the multi-axis campaign affordable. The contribution of this work
is the \textbf{reliability characterization} of microscaling DNNs --- not a faster injection
method.}

\subsection{Expected outcomes}

\begin{itemize}
\item A per-site reliability characterization showing shared-scale faults have a
significantly different (typically larger) impact than element faults, consistent with their
block-wide blast radius.
\item Evidence that reliability varies substantially across microscaling formats even at
comparable inference accuracy --- so accuracy alone is not a reliability proxy.
\item A block-size reliability trade-off: smaller blocks localize scale-fault damage at some
storage/efficiency cost.
\item Per-tensor and per-location sensitivity maps identifying which bits (scale vs.\
element; MSB vs.\ LSB) and which layers most need hardware protection.
\item Design guidance: which microscaling configurations are most fault-robust for a given
accuracy budget.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\emph{Open design choices (settled empirically): learned vs.\ exact interval
characterization for the narrow element formats; single-bit vs.\ multi-bit (H-model) fault
occurrence; and the block-size / format grid actually swept, bounded by available compute.}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

This section collects the definitions the rest of the report relies on.

\subsection{The element formats}

The scale uses the E8M0 encoding: an unsigned power of two with bias 127, where code 255 is
reserved for NaN. Each element is a small sign--exponent--mantissa number. For a nonzero
exponent field $e$ it decodes to $(-1)^{s}(1+f/2^{M})\,2^{e-\mathrm{bias}}$, where $f$ is the
$M$-bit mantissa field. Table~\ref{tab:formats} lists the five element formats. The one
property that turns out to matter most is in the last column.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\small
\caption{MX element formats. The two 8-bit formats reserve codes for NaN or infinity. The
6- and 4-bit formats do not, so every one of their bit patterns is a finite number.}
\label{tab:formats}
\begin{tabular}{llcccc}
\toprule
format & family & sign/exp./mantissa bits & bias & largest value & NaN/Inf codes \\
\midrule
e4m3 & MXFP8 & 1/4/3 & 7  & 448    & 2 (NaN) \\
e5m2 & MXFP8 & 1/5/2 & 15 & 57{,}344 & 8 (2 Inf, 6 NaN) \\
e3m2 & MXFP6 & 1/3/2 & 3  & 28     & none \\
e2m3 & MXFP6 & 1/2/3 & 1  & 7.5    & none \\
e2m1 & MXFP4 & 1/2/1 & 1  & 6      & none \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The OCP specification chooses the scale so that the largest value in a block lands in the
top binade of the element format, $X=\lfloor\log_2\max_i|v_i|\rfloor-e_{\max}$. Since the
largest representable element sits below the top of that binade, the block maximum can be
clipped by up to $12.5\%$ (e4m3, e5m2, e3m2), $6.25\%$ (e2m3) or $25\%$ (e2m1). As a control
I also implemented a non-clipping rule, which I call \emph{fit}: the smallest scale at which
the block maximum is representable. All results use the OCP rule unless stated otherwise.

\subsection{Two fault sites}

Figure~\ref{fig:block} shows the two sites from the brief. An \textbf{element fault} flips one
bit of one code: a mantissa bit nudges the value, an exponent bit multiplies it by a power of
two, and the sign bit negates it. In e4m3 and e5m2 a flip can also turn the code into NaN or
infinity. A \textbf{scale fault} flips bit $j$ of $X$, which multiplies all $K$ values by
$2^{\pm2^{j}}$ at once, and turns the whole block into NaN if it produces code 255.

A short calculation already suggests the scale will matter. A block occupies $8+Kw$ bits, of
which only 8 belong to the scale, about 3\% for $K=32$ and $w=8$. If every stored bit is
equally likely to flip, the expected number of corrupted \emph{values} per block is $8K$
from scale faults and $Kw$ from element faults, a ratio of $8:w$. So an 8-bit format
receives about half of its corrupted values through the 3\% of storage that holds scales,
and a 4-bit format about two thirds.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Let $D$ be a fixed evaluation set of $N$ images, $\hat y_i$ the prediction of the
fault-free, MX-quantised network (the \emph{golden} prediction) and $\hat y_i^{\varphi}$ the
prediction under fault $\varphi$. Two metrics run through the story:

\begin{itemize}
\item the \textbf{any-image SDC rate}, the fraction of faults that change at least one of the
$N$ predictions. This is the conventional metric, and the one I used for the first three
weeks;
\item the \textbf{per-inference rate}, the average over faults of the fraction of the $N$
predictions that change. It answers the question a system designer asks: given one fault,
how likely is it that a particular inference is wrong?
\end{itemize}

\noindent Section~\ref{sec:phase-metric} explains why the second replaced the first. I also
track the \textbf{non-finite rate}, the fraction of faults that produce a NaN or infinity in
the output. Proportions carry Wilson 95\% intervals~\cite{wilson1927}, and per-inference
rates carry normal intervals on the mean over faults.

\subsection{Related work}

Fault-injection studies have shown that errors in DNNs propagate differently by layer, data
type and bit position~\cite{li2017understanding}, and tools such as
Ares~\cite{reagen2018ares} and PyTorchFI~\cite{mahmoud2020pytorchfi} made such campaigns
routine for FP32 and integer models. Bit-flip attacks~\cite{hong2019terminal,rakin2019bfa}
show how few flips it can take to destroy accuracy. Statistical fault injection bounds the
error of a sampled campaign~\cite{leveugle2009sfi}, and TreeFI~\cite{treefi2026} lowers its
cost for FP32 by learning value intervals with similar bit-flip behaviour. None of these
model a shared scale, which is the gap the brief identifies. MX formats themselves are
described in~\cite{rouhani2023mx,ocp2023mx}.

\section{The work, in the order it was done}
\label{sec:work}

\subsection{Getting started: scope, hardware and the tool (9--10 September)}

\paragraph{Scope.} Two drafts existed when I began. One framed the project as a
characterisation study, and is the brief reproduced above. The other framed it as a new
sampling method, an extension of TreeFI to MX formats. The first decision was to commit to
the characterisation. TreeFI stayed in the project only as prior work and as a source of
ideas for keeping campaigns affordable, exactly as the brief's ``supporting tool'' box says.

\paragraph{Hardware.} My machine is a 4-core laptop with no CUDA GPU. That ruled out
DeiT on ImageNet locally: one pass over the ImageNet validation set would take about half an
hour, and a campaign needs thousands of passes. I decided to start at CIFAR scale with ResNet8 and RepVGG-A0, and to keep
the code portable so that heavier runs could move to a GPU later. Before planning
anything I measured the real cost of an injection: 47\,ms on 100 images on four CPU threads.
At that speed the \emph{entire} MXFP8 weight bit space of ResNet8, $638{,}624$ faults, is
about a 16-hour run. My first guess had been months, because I had assumed every fault
would be scored on the full 10{,}000-image test set. Knowing that exhaustive ground truth was
affordable changed what the project could promise.

\paragraph{The tool.} I wrote \texttt{mxfi}, a fault-injection library for MX formats. Its
core (formats, codec, fault model, sampling, statistics) is plain \texttt{numpy} with no deep
learning dependency, and a separate layer connects it to PyTorch models. Three design
decisions keep the injection faithful to what an accelerator executes:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Deployment form.} Batch normalisation is folded into the preceding convolution,
and RepVGG's branches are fused, before quantisation. An unfolded batch norm would
re-normalise whatever a corrupted convolution produced and hide part of the damage.
\item \textbf{Code-domain injection.} Tensors are encoded into real MX bit patterns, the
fault is applied to the stored code, and only then is the block decoded. This is what lets
a scale fault perturb all $K$ values, and lets an element fault land on a NaN code.
\item \textbf{Golden-versus-faulty comparison} on a fixed, class-balanced subset of 200 test
images, recording for each fault how many predictions changed and whether the output went
non-finite.
\end{enumerate}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:campaign} shows a weight campaign. Activation faults go through forward
hooks that quantise a layer's input to MX, flip one bit of one image's encoding, and decode.
Weight faults are persistent: every inference reads the corrupted weight until it is
rewritten. Activation faults are transient and affect one inference.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\small
\caption{Weight fault-injection campaign in \texttt{mxfi}}
\label{alg:campaign}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require network $M$, format $F$, block size $K$, evaluation set $D$, budget $n$
\State $M\gets\textsc{Deploy}(M)$ \Comment{fold batch norm, fuse branches}
\ForAll{quantisable layers $\ell$}
  \State view $W_\ell$ as (outputs $\times$ reduction); split the reduction axis into blocks of $K$
  \State $X_\ell\gets$ per-block scale (OCP rule);\; $C_\ell\gets\mathrm{round}_F(W_\ell\, 2^{-X_\ell})$
  \State $W_\ell\gets\textsc{Decode}(X_\ell,C_\ell)$ \Comment{the network now holds MX values}
\EndFor
\State $\hat y\gets\arg\max M(D)$ \Comment{golden predictions}
\For{$t=1$ to $n$}
  \State draw $\varphi=(\ell,\mathrm{site},\mathrm{index},b)$ uniformly over all stored bits
  \If{site $=$ element} $C_\ell[\mathrm{index}]\gets C_\ell[\mathrm{index}]\oplus 2^{b}$; re-decode that one weight
  \Else{}\; $X_\ell[\mathrm{index}]\gets X_\ell[\mathrm{index}]\oplus 2^{b}$; re-decode the $K$ weights of the block
  \EndIf
  \State $\hat y^{\varphi}\gets\arg\max M(D)$; record which images changed and non-finiteness; restore
\EndFor
\State \Return rates over all faults with confidence intervals
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\noindent Replacing line 9 with an enumeration of every stored bit makes the campaign
\emph{exhaustive}: it returns the true failure rate with no sampling error at all.

\paragraph{The first network.} ResNet8, the MLPerf Tiny image-classification
model~\cite{banbury2021mlperftiny,he2016resnet}, trained on CIFAR-10 to $87.01\%$ top-1, which
matches the reference. Training took 82 seconds per epoch on the laptop.

\subsection{First campaigns on ResNet8 (10--11 September)}

The first campaigns were 3000 uniformly sampled weight faults per format at $K=32$, scored
with the any-image metric. Several things showed up at once.

\paragraph{The hypothesis looked right immediately.} e5m2 and e3m2 differed in accuracy by
0.05 points but in fault vulnerability by $1.77\times$, with disjoint confidence intervals
(Table~\ref{tab:first}). Accuracy was clearly not a reliability proxy. Interestingly, the
6-bit format looked \emph{worse}. That direction later turned out to be an artefact of the
metric (Section~\ref{sec:phase-metric}), but the larger point, that equal accuracy does not
mean equal reliability, survived everything that came after.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\small
\caption{The first campaign (ResNet8, CIFAR-10, $K=32$, 3000 faults each, any-image metric).
The direction between e5m2 and e3m2 was later reversed under the per-inference metric.}
\label{tab:first}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
format & bits & accuracy & any-image SDC [95\% CI] & non-finite outputs \\
\midrule
e5m2 & 8 & 84.80\% & 28.9\% [27.3, 30.6] & 7.7\% \\
e3m2 & 6 & 84.85\% & 51.3\% [49.5, 53.1] & 0.0\% \\

\bottomrule

\paragraph{The failure modes were different, not just the rates.} e5m2 failed rarely but
catastrophically: $7.7\%$ of its faults produced NaN or infinity at the output, because it has
special codes and a wide exponent. e3m2 never produced a non-finite output, as a format
without special codes cannot. This was the first hint of what became the central mechanism of
the study.

\paragraph{The severity measure in the brief did not work.} The brief scores an element fault
by $d=|\log v'-\log v|$. Over 8374 element faults its rank correlation with observed failure
was $0.026$, effectively zero. The reason is in the definition. Negating a value leaves $|v|$
unchanged, so a sign flip scores zero severity, yet sign flips were the most damaging bit
position in the sample. A sampler built on this measure would have given the most dangerous
bit almost no budget. Finding a measure that works became a thread I came back to later
(Section~\ref{sec:phase-puzzle}).

\paragraph{Scale faults dominated.} Scale faults were 2--3 times more likely than element
faults to change a prediction, and they changed 37--41 of the 200 predictions on average,
against 0.7--15.6 for element faults. Between 9\% and 13\% of them produced non-finite
outputs. But scale bits are only $3.1\%$ of storage, so a uniform campaign of 3000 drew only
about 100 of them, and the most important stratum was the least measured.

\paragraph{Two smaller observations.} Under the OCP scale rule the 6-bit e2m3 was more
accurate than the 8-bit e4m3, and the order flipped back under the non-clipping rule, so the
rule itself could distort a format comparison. And accuracy moved by about one point or less
across block sizes 8 to 64, which meant block size could be studied as a reliability axis
without an accuracy confound.

\subsection{A first attempt at cheaper campaigns (11 September)}

The shortage of scale samples suggested trying stratified sampling. I compared three
allocations at the same budget of 3000: uniform, Neyman allocation over layer and site, and
Neyman allocation with an extra weight $\Phi$ for blast radius, an idea from the TreeFI-style
draft. All three estimators agreed on the model-wide rate to within $0.003$, so the weighting
was correct. The $\Phi$ weight moved $36\%$ of the budget to scale bits and cut the
scale-stratum interval by a factor of $10.5$ in variance. It also widened the model-wide
interval, though, so it was a trade between which estimate is precise, not a free gain.
Plain layer-by-site stratification gave $0.94\times$, slightly worse than uniform, because
per-layer failure rates were too similar for Neyman allocation to exploit.

The lesson was that the variance does not live across layers. It lives within the value
distribution, which is where TreeFI looks. I set sampling aside at this point and returned to
it once I had ground truth to test against.

\subsection{Weights, activations and block size (11--12 September)}

\paragraph{Comparing weights with activations needs care.} A weight fault is exposed to all
200 inferences of a campaign, but an activation fault can only ever affect the one inference
it lands in. Counting ``did any image change'' therefore understates activation faults by
about two orders of magnitude. The fair quantity is the chance that a given inference is
corrupted, which is the per-inference rate of Section~\ref{sec:metrics}. I introduced it here
for activations only. It took me another two weeks to see that it should replace the
any-image metric everywhere. (The activation numbers measured in this phase were also wrong,
because of a codec bug I found only at the end of the project; see
Section~\ref{sec:phase-c100}. The normalisation argument stands.)

\paragraph{Block size is a genuine trade.} Raising $K$ from 8 to 64 cuts the share of storage
taken by scales from $11.1\%$ to $1.7\%$, so a scale is less likely to be hit. But each scale
fault becomes worse, because it now affects more values (Table~\ref{tab:block}). The two
sites move in opposite directions along this axis, which is why block size cannot be tuned on
a single aggregate number.

\paragraph{The OCP rule and block size.} Injecting one fixed set of element faults at every
block size, the any-image rate swung by $2.2\times$ non-monotonically under the OCP rule but
stayed flat under the non-clipping rule. I concluded at the time that OCP clipping
\emph{manufactures} a block-size trend. Rescored per inference two weeks later, this turned
out to be overstated: there is a real, monotone trend under both rules (the last row of
Table~\ref{tab:block}), and the OCP rule only exaggerates it under the any-image metric.

\subsection{A second architecture, and the first mechanism (12--13 September)}

\paragraph{RepVGG-A0.} I trained RepVGG-A0~\cite{ding2021repvgg} (7.0M parameters after
fusion, $91.44\%$ top-1) and repeated the campaigns. The matched-accuracy result replicated,
but its direction reversed: e3m2, which had looked $1.77\times$ worse than e5m2 on ResNet8,
was now $7.2\times$ safer. My conclusion at the time was that a format ranking should never be
quoted from a single architecture. That warning was sound, but for the wrong reason, as
Section~\ref{sec:phase-metric} explains.

\paragraph{Why the failure mode shifts with model size.} The non-finite share explained the
reversal. On RepVGG, \emph{all 158} of e5m2's element failures were non-finite events. Not a
single ordinary perturbation changed a prediction. On ResNet8 only $28\%$ were. My
explanation was that a larger network absorbs ordinary perturbations that a small one cannot,
but nothing absorbs a NaN, and only formats with special codes can produce one. So as
capacity grows, vulnerability stops depending on how much a flip perturbs a value and starts
depending on whether the format can represent NaN at all. This made two predictions: that the
effect should be graded by the number of special codes, and that a Transformer, being
accurate enough to ignore small perturbations, should show it sharply.

Scale dominance also strengthened with size: the scale-to-element ratio for e4m3 went from
$3.0\times$ on ResNet8 to $17.8\times$ on RepVGG, because capacity absorbs a single corrupted
weight but not 32 of them at once.

\paragraph{Isolating capacity.} RepVGG and ResNet8 differ in depth, topology, training budget
and accuracy at once, so to test the capacity explanation properly I trained ResNet8 at five
widths (8 to 48) with identical topology, data and a 30-epoch budget. That spans a $35\times$
range of parameters ($20$k to $692$k) and accuracies from $80.0\%$ to $89.6\%$. On the first
seed, the benefit of capacity ranked exactly as predicted, e3m2 most and e5m2 least, and the
non-finite rate stayed flat with width while the non-finite share of failures rose. But the
curves between the endpoints were badly non-monotone, and each width was one trained model.
The clear next step was more seeds.

\subsection{More seeds, and a puzzle that exposed the metric (17--20 September)}

I wrote a restartable script to retrain every width under further seeds. With two seeds the
predicted three-way ordering held in only one of them. The spread between two networks of the
same width, differing only in their seed, was a median $12.8\times$ the uncertainty from the
3000 injections. More injections would not have helped; only more trained networks could.
That is a general lesson about fault-injection studies: a result quoted from one trained
network is much less certain than its confidence interval suggests.

One cell refused to behave: e4m3 at width 32 was anomalously robust in \emph{both} seeds. To
find out why, I injected the same 60 sign flips into the same width-32 network under each
format. The flips moved the logits by the same amount in every format (median shift 0.12),
yet changed a prediction in 1 of 60 cases under e4m3 and 26 of 60 under e3m2. The difference
was in the golden network, not in the faults. The two e4m3 width-32 networks were the only
ones of 39 with no evaluation image near a decision boundary (no top-1 versus top-2 margin
below 0.2). Every other network had at least one such knife-edge image, which flips under
almost any nudge. Across all 39 networks, the any-image rate tracked the number of
near-tie images at Spearman $+0.89$.

This was the first clear sign that the any-image metric can measure the evaluation set rather
than the fault. I did not yet see how far the problem reached. Since the campaign files stored
only how many images changed and not which ones, I let the running sweep finish unchanged and
changed the campaign code afterwards to record the change set of every fault.

\subsection{Moving to the GPU cluster: ground truth, ten seeds and a Transformer (20--22 September)}

At this point I got access to an IIT Kanpur server with NVIDIA A100 GPUs. It has no internet
access and no copy of ImageNet, which mattered later, but it made three things possible.

\paragraph{Exhaustive ground truth.} I flipped every one of the $638{,}624$ bits of a
ResNet8's e4m3 weight storage and scored each one, at about 170 injections per second, in a
little over an hour. The true any-image rate was $0.4171$. An ordinary 3000-fault campaign
had returned $0.4270$ with interval $[0.4094, 0.4448]$: within $2.4\%$ of the truth, with
the interval covering it. Every sampled number in the study rests on that assumption, and now
it was checked rather than assumed. The exact enumeration also showed that $94.4\%$ of all
possible scale flips change at least one prediction, and that a scale fault changes $49.9$ of
200 predictions on average against $3.3$ for an element fault, a $15\times$ difference.

The exact per-layer picture was a surprise (Figure~\ref{fig:layers}). Vulnerability runs
\emph{opposite} to layer size. The 3584-bit stem convolution is the most fragile layer,
because its errors propagate through the whole network, and the $304{,}128$-bit final
convolution is the most robust. A uniform campaign samples layers in proportion to their size,
so it spends $48\%$ of its budget on the most robust layer and $0.6\%$ on the most fragile.

\paragraph{Ten seeds per width.} I retrained the whole width sweep on the GPU with ten seeds
per width: 50 networks, 150 campaigns, $450{,}000$ injections. Fitting the capacity slope
$\mathrm{d}\log(\text{failure rate})/\mathrm{d}\log(\text{parameters})$ for each network and
comparing formats within the same network, both adjacent pairs now separated ($t=4.00$ and
$4.01$, $p\approx0.003$ each) and the slopes ranked exactly by special-code count. The
non-finite rate stayed a property of the format across the whole $35\times$ range (e5m2
varied by $1.08\times$, e4m3 by $1.20\times$, e3m2 was exactly zero everywhere). The simpler
endpoint-ratio test held in only 5 of 10 seeds, which I took as a reminder to use the
statistic with the most power and to say so.

\paragraph{A vision Transformer.} I trained a small vision Transformer~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit}
with the width and head count of DeiT-Tiny~\cite{touvron2021deit}, depth 6 and $4\times4$
patches (2.7M parameters, $80.4$--$81.9\%$ on CIFAR-10, three seeds). Every weight matrix is a
linear layer, so MX blocks run along the model dimension, and LayerNorm stays unquantised, as
in deployment. This gave the cleanest matched-accuracy comparison of the project: e5m2 and
e3m2 differed by $0.01$ points of accuracy, yet e5m2 was $17.8\times$ more vulnerable on the
any-image metric, and $94\%$ of its element failures were non-finite. The prediction from the
CNNs held, and more sharply. Neither LayerNorm nor softmax masked the damage, and scale
faults dominated here too.

One result did not fit. e3m2 and e2m3 are both 6-bit, both free of special codes and equally
accurate, yet differed by $5.8\times$ in element failures. Special codes could not explain
that.

\subsection{Chasing the 6-bit puzzle (22--25 September)}
\label{sec:phase-puzzle}

\paragraph{Ruling things out.} The gap was solid (Fisher exact $p=2.5\times10^{-30}$), so I
tested explanations one by one. It was not the size of the perturbation: e2m3 actually
perturbs weights \emph{less} than e3m2, and within matched perturbation-size bins it still
failed $7\times$ more often. It was not which weights were hit, and it was not a difference in
margins, since the golden margin distributions were indistinguishable.

\paragraph{The fragility belongs to the network.} I then perturbed single weights by a fixed
multiple of the layer's RMS value, a probe with no bit patterns in it at all. At $1.5\times$
RMS, in 1500 trials, it never changed a prediction of the e3m2 or e5m2 networks, but changed
the e4m3 network's in $2$--$5\%$ of trials. So two quantised networks of equal accuracy could
differ by more than an order of magnitude in how much a weight perturbation of a given size
disturbs them. Among the 6- and 8-bit formats, the ones with 2 mantissa bits looked $6$--$8\times$
safer than the ones with 3, and I first wrote this up as ``mantissa width tracks
vulnerability''.

\paragraph{A sensitivity measure.} To explain it I defined a per-weight sensitivity
$s_i=|\partial m/\partial w_i|\cdot\mathrm{rms}/m$, where $m$ is the margin between the top two
logits. A perturbation of $\delta\cdot\mathrm{rms}$ should flip a prediction roughly when
$s_i\delta>1$. Computed by backpropagation, $s$ predicted the probe at correlation $+0.998$.
Extending the comparison to all five formats then broke my mantissa explanation: e2m1, with
the fewest mantissa bits, was the \emph{most} vulnerable. Across formats, sensitivity predicted
the perturbation-driven failure rate at $r=+0.93$, while mantissa width correlated at $-0.25$.
Mantissa width had been a correlate within one group of formats, not a cause.

\paragraph{A severity measure that predicts failure.} The same sensitivity gave me a
replacement for the brief's severity measure. For a fault that changes a weight from $v$ to
$v'$,
\[
\mathrm{margin\_shift}_i = s_i\,\frac{|v_i'-v_i|}{\mathrm{rms}}
\]
is the fraction of the decision margin the fault uses up. Over $39{,}337$ element faults on the
Transformer, the brief's $d$ had AUC $0.435$, slightly worse than chance, relative error
reached $0.699$, absolute change in RMS units $0.845$, and $\mathrm{margin\_shift}$ reached
$0.990$ (Figure~\ref{fig:severity}). Its threshold needs no tuning: flagging faults with
$\mathrm{margin\_shift}>1$ catches $98.5\%$ of all failures while flagging only $4.4\%$ of the
fault space.

\paragraph{Firming up.} In the same days I ran a second exhaustive campaign, ResNet8 under
e3m2 ($483{,}904$ injections), in which not one fault produced a non-finite value, and the
sampled estimate again covered the truth ($0.1070$ against $0.1079$). I retrained RepVGG with
three seeds, and the ordering e3m2 $<$ e4m3 $<$ e5m2 held on all of them. I also tested
value-aware sampling against the two exhaustive campaigns for the first time: splitting the
fault space by $\mathrm{margin\_shift}$ gave a $7\times$ variance reduction on e3m2 and
$2.3\times$ on e4m3, for the any-image rate.

\paragraph{What $s$ really measures.} One question remained: why does $s$ differ between
formats at all? I first guessed that it came from how inputs and gradients line up for
particular weights, and tested this with a permutation that destroys that alignment while
keeping every value. The sensitivity tail moved by at most $2\%$, so the guess was wrong.
Splitting $s$ into its two factors gave the answer. The gradient factor agrees across all five
formats to within $1.08\times$. The whole $10\times$ spread comes from the margin in the
denominator, and specifically from the \emph{one} evaluation image closest to a decision
boundary. And that image belongs to the evaluation set rather than to the network: $1/\min m$
ranked fifteen networks by failure rate at Spearman $+0.98$ on the campaign's own 200 images,
but only $+0.21$ on 2000 fresh images. This was the near-tie effect from the width sweep again,
now explaining a format comparison.

\subsection{Two mistakes caught, and a change of metric (25--27 September)}
\label{sec:phase-metric}

\paragraph{Checking what the campaigns were run on.} Training is not bit-reproducible across
machines, and the same checkpoint name on my laptop and on the cluster turned out to hold
different weights. None of the 24 shared checkpoints matched, while accuracies agreed to a few
tenths of a percent, so nothing had looked wrong. I wrote a check that replays a sample of a
campaign's recorded faults against a checkpoint and compares outcomes. It showed that the
e4m3 and e3m2 exhaustive campaigns had been recorded on \emph{two different} trained networks
(300/300 agreement with one, $202$--$253$/300 with the other). So the ``exact'' comparison
between the two formats was really one network against another, and given the tenfold spread
between networks it said nothing about the formats. The first version of the e4m3 sampling
result had the same problem. Since then every campaign is tied to the checkpoint that produced
it, the replay check runs automatically, and the two exhaustive campaigns are used as two
independent references, never as a format comparison.

\paragraph{The metric itself.} To redo the comparison properly I replayed one fixed list of
1500 element faults per format against nine disjoint, class-balanced sets of 200 images, on
each of two networks. Under the any-image metric, e3m2 and e4m3 looked equal ($1.03\times$ and
$0.93\times$), their order reversed from one image set to the next, and the rate moved by up to
seven times its own confidence-interval width between sets, tracking each set's smallest
margin (Spearman $+0.80$ to $+0.93$). Scored per inference, the same faults on the same image
sets gave $5.6\times$ and $7.6\times$ between the formats, stable to within
$1.15$--$1.27\times$ across image sets.

The any-image metric saturates: beyond a few dozen images it mostly reports whether the set
contains a near-tie input. The effect was real, and the conventional metric was hiding it.
From here on, the per-inference rate is the primary metric of the study.

\paragraph{Rescoring everything.} Because every campaign had always recorded the number of
changed predictions per fault, all 119 weight campaigns ($1.44$ million faults on eight
model configurations) could be rescored per inference without a single new injection.

under the any-image metric. The ``direction reverses across architectures'' finding of
12 September was a metric artefact. On the Transformer, a single e5m2 element fault is about
$2000$ times as likely as an e3m2 one to corrupt a given inference, at $0.01$ points of
accuracy difference.

\paragraph{Value-aware sampling, re-derived.} The sampling gains measured earlier were for the
any-image rate, so I re-derived them for the per-inference rate. The target changes shape:
$89.2\%$ of ResNet8's e3m2 faults corrupt no inference at all, and almost all of the variance
sits in a thin tail. The estimator changes too. A per-inference rate is a mean rather than a
proportion, so each stratum's variance has to be estimated from a pilot instead of assumed to
be $p(1-p)$. I scored the whole fault space with $\mathrm{margin\_shift}$, which costs only
gradients, cut it into six strata at its 50th, 75th, 90th, 97th and 99.5th percentiles, spent
a fifth of the budget on a pilot, and allocated the rest by Neyman
allocation~\cite{neyman1934}. Replayed against both exhaustive campaigns, the variance fell
$57$--$120\times$ against uniform sampling on both formats, with no measurable bias. A
3000-injection stratified campaign is as precise as a uniform one of $171{,}000$ (e4m3) to
$346{,}000$ (e3m2) injections.

\subsection{Settling the brief's two open design choices (28 September -- 1 October)}

\paragraph{Single-bit or multi-bit?} The brief adopted single-bit flips and left the
multi-bit H-model as an extension. To settle it, I sampled 2000 stored words per format on
each of two ResNet8 networks and injected \emph{every} single- and double-bit pattern of each
word, $390{,}000$ injections in all, recording which predictions changed. A double flip
corrupts $1.04$--$1.55\times$ as many inferences as a single flip, and an adjacent pair, the
pattern a multi-cell upset produces, $0.85$--$1.13\times$. The format ordering and scale
dominance are unchanged. Double flips are sub-additive, partly because a second flip can move
a word \emph{off} the NaN code its partner would have reached, and partly because two scale
bits of opposite value cancel. Plugging the measured rates into the H-model's own expansion,
doubles within a word reach $1\%$ of the expected damage only at a per-bit flip probability of
$2$--$3\times10^{-3}$. At that rate ResNet8 already holds more than a thousand flipped bits at
once, so the one-fault-per-inference assumption fails through accumulation across words long
before multiplicity within a word matters. Single-bit injection is sufficient.

\paragraph{Learned intervals or exact enumeration?} The brief also asked whether fault impact
should be characterised as TreeFI does, with value intervals learned by a regression tree from
a pilot, or by exact enumeration over the MX code table. An MX element alphabet has at most
256 codes, so the decoded change of every (code, bit) pair can be tabulated in closed form at
no injection cost. Replayed against both exhaustive campaigns, strata built from the exact
$|v'-v|$ alone cut variance $48$--$65\times$. Strata learned from a $20\%$ pilot never beat
uniform sampling ($0.10$--$0.98\times$). Two things defeat the learner. A pilot of a few
hundred faults, on a target where $89\%$ of faults do nothing, sees too few failures to place
its strata. And in e4m3 the damaging tail is not a range of values at all. It is the discrete
set of (code, bit) pairs that land on NaN, which a code table lists exactly. Interval learning
solves a problem that FP32 has, an alphabet of $2^{32}$ values, and that MX does not.

\paragraph{The sign bit, revisited.} The exhaustive any-image ranking had made the sign bit
the worst element bit, which was the sharpest point against the brief's severity measure.
Rescored per inference (Figure~\ref{fig:perbit}) the picture is more interesting. Among faults
that leave the output finite, the sign bit is still the worst. In total damage, though, it
ranks seventh of eight in e4m3. The $1.27\%$ of element faults that flip a code into NaN each
corrupt every inference, and together they carry $78\%$ of all element damage. A sign flip
never touches the magnitude bits, so it is the one flip that can never reach the NaN code.
The scale bits tell a similar story. On this network the scale bytes lie between 114 and 120,
so bits 4--6 are always set and bit 3 almost never. Flipping bit 3 multiplies a block by 256
and corrupts $75\%$ of inferences, and flipping bit 7 overflows it ($98\%$), while flipping bits
4--6 shrinks the block towards zero and corrupts only $3\%$. Growing a block is catastrophic;
shrinking it is mild.

\subsection{A read-side guard for special codes (1--2 October)}

If most element damage comes from one transition, into a special code, there is an obvious
defence: decode any special element code as zero when it is read. A fault that would have
produced NaN then only zeroes one weight. I tested this on all three architectures under
e4m3 and e5m2. Every element fault that lands on a special code was found by enumeration, so
its share of the fault space is exact, and a sample of them was injected with and without the
guard. The sampled estimate matched the exhaustive campaign to four significant figures
($0.016357\to0.003749$ against $0.016354\to0.003747$).

The result is in Table~\ref{tab:guard}. On RepVGG and the Transformer, where special-code
faults carry $97$--$100\%$ of element damage, the guard cuts the element per-inference rate by
at least $29\times$ and up to $4100\times$, bringing e5m2 down to the level of e3m2. On
ResNet8 the reduction is smaller, $3.9$--$53\times$, because a small network is also fragile
to ordinary perturbations, which the guard does not touch. No guarded fault produced a
non-finite output. The guard was simulated in software; in hardware it is one comparator on
the read path. It turns ``prefer formats without special codes'' from a constraint into a
choice.

\subsection{A bug in my own codec, and a harder dataset (2--3 October)}
\label{sec:phase-c100}

\paragraph{The codec bug.} While preparing the activation experiments for CIFAR-100 I noticed
that no activation campaign, on any model, had ever recorded a non-finite output, even though
enumeration showed that activations have the same share of special-code faults as weights.
The cause was my codec: it mapped a NaN input to zero. An activation-quantised layer
re-encodes its input, so a NaN created by an activation fault was quietly erased at the next
layer. The OCP conversion does the opposite. It takes the shared scale from the block maximum,
so a NaN anywhere in a block makes the whole block NaN. I fixed the codec to do exactly that,
added a test that pins the behaviour, and reran every activation campaign on 100 images with
4000 faults each. Weight campaigns were unaffected, since trained weights contain no NaN to
re-encode.

The corrected numbers (Table~\ref{tab:act}) withdraw my earlier claim that activation faults
barely matter on the larger models. Per inference, an element fault in a weight does
$0.6$--$2.2\times$ the damage of one in an activation, and under e4m3 the two are
statistically indistinguishable on CIFAR-10. The reason is the same in both tensors: about 1\%
of faults land on a special code and destroy the inference. The real difference between
weights and activations is \emph{exposure}: a weight fault persists across every inference
until the memory is rewritten, while an activation fault lives for one. Scale faults dominate
in both, $14\times$ in activations and $11\times$ in weights on ResNet8.

\paragraph{CIFAR-100 in place of ImageNet.} The brief's Transformer study was DeiT on ImageNet,
and ImageNet was not available on any machine I could use. CIFAR-100 was the nearest
affordable substitute: ten times the classes and a tenth of the images per class, so networks
sit much closer to their decision boundaries, which is exactly the property the margin results
had shown to matter. I retrained all three architectures with unchanged recipes, three seeds
each (ResNet8 $58\%$, RepVGG-A0 $70$--$71\%$, ViT $47$--$49\%$).

Every headline replicated. Formats were matched in accuracy (four formats within $0.01$ points
on the ViT), the ordering e3m2 $<$ e4m3 $<$ e5m2 held on all nine networks, scale faults
outweighed element faults everywhere ($1.7\times$ to $3600\times$), and NaN codes carried
$94$--$100\%$ of e4m3 and e5m2 element damage on the larger models.

What changed was the floor (Table~\ref{tab:c100}). With smaller margins, the formats without
special codes became $3$--$22\times$ more vulnerable, while e4m3 and e5m2 barely moved, since a
NaN corrupts an inference however wide the margins are. The gap between formats therefore
narrows: on the ViT the e4m3/e3m2 ratio falls from $461\times$ to $19\times$. The advantage of
NaN-free formats is still large, but it is smallest on exactly the harder tasks that real
deployments face. The read-side guard still works, with reductions of at least
$13$--$1387\times$ on RepVGG and the ViT. Among the NaN-free formats the ordering does not
carry over: on CIFAR-100 the ViT ranks e2m1 as the safest of the three, which is what the
sensitivity result predicts, since perturbation damage follows the network and not the field
widths.

\noindent Three lessons stand out, and I would apply them to any fault-injection study:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Score per inference.} The any-image metric depends on whether the evaluation set
happens to contain a near-tie input, and it hid or reversed real effects four times.
\item \textbf{Replicate over trained networks, not only over injections.} The spread between
networks of the same architecture was about ten times the uncertainty from 3000 injections.
\item \textbf{Tie every campaign to its exact checkpoint, and check the plumbing.} A
replay check would have caught the checkpoint mismatch at once, and an absent non-finite
output where one was expected is what exposed the codec bug.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Design guidance}
\label{sec:guidance}

For designers of MX accelerators:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Protect the shared scale first.} A scale fault typically does $13\times$ to more
than $1000\times$ the damage of an element fault, and scales are only $2$--$11\%$ of storage.
Parity or ECC on scale bytes is the cheapest protection with the largest payoff. If only some
scale bits can be protected, protect those normally at 0, whose flips grow the block.
\item \textbf{Neutralise special codes.} Prefer element formats without NaN/Inf codes, or decode
special element codes as zero on read. The guard removes most of the element vulnerability of
e4m3 and e5m2 on the larger models.
\item \textbf{Do not judge reliability by accuracy or bit width.} Formats of equal accuracy
differ by orders of magnitude, and among NaN-free formats the ranking depends on the network
and task.
\item \textbf{Treat block size as a trade-off.} Larger blocks save scale storage but make each
scale fault worse. With protected scales, large blocks are safe.
\item \textbf{Prioritise early layers.} The small stem layer is the most fragile; protection
should follow vulnerability, not storage size.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent For anyone running fault-injection campaigns on MX models:
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item Report the per-inference rate, not the any-image SDC rate.
\item Inject single-bit faults; double-bit faults add no first-order information.
\item Stratify by exact per-code enumeration of the decoded change, weighted by gradient
sensitivity where available, for $57$--$120\times$ fewer injections at the same precision.
\item Tie every campaign to its exact checkpoint, and verify it by replaying recorded faults.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{ImageNet and DeiT were not evaluated.} ImageNet was not reachable from any machine
available to me, so CIFAR-100 stood in for it, with a small vision Transformer trained from
scratch at DeiT-Tiny width. That network reaches only $47$--$49\%$ on CIFAR-100, far below a
pretrained DeiT on ImageNet. The CIFAR-100 study shows that the conclusions survive a harder
task with smaller margins; it is not a measurement of DeiT on ImageNet. The trend it reveals,
a narrowing gap between formats as margins shrink, suggests that absolute gaps at ImageNet
scale may be smaller than on CIFAR-10. The directions of the effects, which held on every
network, are the more transferable result.

\paragraph{Model scale.} The largest network has 7 million parameters. The optional small LLM
and MXINT8 were not studied. The capacity results suggest that larger models absorb
perturbations but not NaNs, which would make special codes even more dominant, but I did not
test this directly.

\paragraph{Fault model.} Only faults in stored values are modelled: weights, activations and
their scales. Datapath, accumulator, control and timing faults are out of scope. Every stored
bit is assumed equally likely to flip ($\kappa\equiv1$ in the brief's notation); a graded
profile would reweight the per-bit results but not change them.

\paragraph{Other.} Campaigns score a fixed subset of 200 images (100 for activations) to keep
millions of injections affordable. The per-inference rate is robust to this choice, but
absolute rates carry the uncertainty of the subset. The NaN guard was evaluated in software,
not in hardware, and the mechanism behind the element-side block-size trend is not
established.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The brief asked whether the reliability of microscaling DNNs varies across formats at
comparable accuracy, and whether faults in the shared scale behave differently from faults in
elements. Both answers are yes, on every network I tested. Formats of equal accuracy differ by
up to three orders of magnitude, and a scale fault is always more damaging than an element
fault, typically by $13\times$ to more than $1000\times$.

The study also found out why. Most element damage comes from the small fraction of flips that
land on NaN or infinity codes. No amount of model capacity absorbs them, and a simple
read-side guard removes them. What is left is governed by the decision margins of the network,
not by how many mantissa bits the format has.

Along the way the project produced a bit-accurate fault-injection library validated against
exhaustive ground truth, showed that the conventional SDC metric can hide the differences it
is meant to reveal, replaced the brief's severity measure with one that predicts failure and
cuts campaign cost by two orders of magnitude, and settled both of the brief's open design
choices. The most useful next step is to repeat the key campaigns on a pretrained Transformer
at ImageNet scale, which this project could not reach.

\paragraph{Code and data.} The \texttt{mxfi} library, every experiment script, the trained
networks and the raw campaign outputs are in the project repository
(\url{https://github.com/peeyushagarwal2004/CS496_UGP}). A detailed log of all 72 findings,
including those later revised or withdrawn, is in \texttt{docs/findings.md}.
