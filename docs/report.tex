\begin{abstract}
\noindent Microscaling (MX) formats store a block of $K$ narrow 4- to 8-bit elements with one
shared 8-bit power-of-two scale. A bit fault can therefore land in an element, corrupting one
value, or in the shared scale, rescaling all $K$ values at once. This report characterises the
reliability of MX-quantised DNNs under such faults. Using a bit-accurate fault-injection library,
more than three million injections were run on ResNet8, RepVGG-A0 and a small vision
Transformer, trained on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, across five element formats, four block sizes,
weights and activations, and every bit position, with two exhaustive campaigns as ground truth.
Formats of equal accuracy differ in fault vulnerability by up to three orders of magnitude, with
e3m2 $<$ e4m3 $<$ e5m2 on every network, and a scale fault is always more damaging than an
element fault, typically by $13\times$ to over $1000\times$. The dominant mechanism is the NaN
and infinity codes of the 8-bit formats: they carry 97--100\% of element damage on the larger
networks, model capacity never absorbs them, and decoding them as zero on read cuts element
damage by $29\times$ or more. The study also shows that the conventional any-image SDC metric is
confounded by near-tie inputs, and that exact enumeration of the MX code table reduces campaign
cost by up to two orders of magnitude.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Keywords:} DNN reliability, microscaling formats, MXFP, fault injection,
shared scale, silent data corruption.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

MX formats~\cite{rouhani2023mx,ocp2023mx} make low-precision inference cheap by letting a block
of $K$ elements share one scale: the stored value is $v_i=\mathrm{decode}(c_i)\cdot2^{X-127}$,
where $c_i$ is a narrow element code and $X$ an 8-bit E8M0 scale. In FP32 every value is a
self-contained word, so a bit fault perturbs exactly one value, and existing DNN fault-injection
work~\cite{li2017understanding,reagen2018ares,mahmoud2020pytorchfi} rests on that assumption. MX
breaks it: a fault can occur at two structurally different sites with very different blast
radii.

\briefbox{CORE HYPOTHESIS}{Reliability of microscaling DNNs varies substantially across formats
\textbf{even at comparable inference accuracy}, and faults in the \textbf{shared scaling factor}
behave very differently from faults in individual elements, because a single scale fault
rescales an entire block of $K$ values at once.}

\noindent The study has five objectives: \textbf{O1} characterise element versus scale fault
propagation; \textbf{O2} compare formats at equal accuracy; \textbf{O3} measure the block-size
trade-off; \textbf{O4} compare weights with activations and fault locations; \textbf{O5}
identify the representation characteristics that drive vulnerability. Two methodological
choices are settled along the way: learned value intervals~\cite{treefi2026} versus exact code
enumeration, and single-bit versus multi-bit injection.

\section{Background}

\paragraph{Formats.} The scale is E8M0: an unsigned power of two with bias 127, code 255 being
NaN. Elements are small sign--exponent--mantissa floats (Table~\ref{tab:formats}). Only the two
8-bit formats reserve bit patterns for NaN or infinity, which turns out to be the property that
matters most. The OCP rule sets $X-127=\lfloor\log_2\max_i|v_i|\rfloor-e_{\max}$, aligning the
block maximum with the format's top binade; since the largest element lies below the top of that
binade, the maximum can be clipped by up to 12.5\% (25\% for e2m1).

\paragraph{Two fault sites.} An \textbf{element fault} flips bit $b$ of one code: a mantissa
bit nudges the value, an exponent bit scales it, the sign bit negates it, and in e4m3/e5m2 the
flip may produce NaN or infinity. A \textbf{scale fault} flips bit $j$ of $X$ and multiplies all
$K$ values by $2^{\pm2^{j}}$; bit 7 shifts the exponent by 128 and overflows float32
(Figure~\ref{fig:block}). Scales are only $8/(8+Kw)$ of storage, about 3\% at $K=32$, $w=8$, yet
under uniform bit upsets they receive $8:w$ of all corrupted values.

\paragraph{Metrics.} For a fault $\varphi$ and a fixed evaluation set of $N$ images, let
$\hat y_i$ be the golden prediction of the fault-free quantised network. The conventional
\emph{any-image SDC rate} is the fraction of faults that change at least one of the $N$
predictions. The \emph{per-inference rate} is the mean over faults of the fraction of the $N$
predictions that change; it answers how likely one fault is to corrupt a given inference.
Section~\ref{sec:metric} shows the any-image rate is confounded by the evaluation set, so
\textbf{all rates below are per inference}. Proportions carry Wilson 95\%
intervals~\cite{wilson1927}.

\section{Methodology}

\paragraph{Injection engine.} The \texttt{mxfi} library encodes each tensor into real MX bit
patterns, with scales and element codes as separately addressable arrays, applies a fault as an
XOR of one bit, and decodes. Because an element alphabet has at most 256 codes, the decoded
change of every (code, bit) flip is also tabulated exactly. Networks are injected in deployment
form (batch norm folded, RepVGG branches fused); weights are blocked along the reduction axis.
Following the OCP conversion, a NaN anywhere in a block makes the block NaN, so NaNs propagate
through activation re-quantisation. Activation faults are injected through forward hooks into
one image's encoding. The library has 214 unit tests.

\paragraph{Networks.} ResNet8 (MLPerf Tiny~\cite{banbury2021mlperftiny}, 78K parameters, also
at five widths spanning 20K--692K with up to ten seeds per width), RepVGG-A0~\cite{ding2021repvgg}
(7.0M fused), and a ViT at DeiT-Tiny width~\cite{touvron2021deit} (2.7M, depth 6), each trained
on CIFAR-10 (87.0\%, 91.4\%, 80--82\%) and CIFAR-100 (58\%, 70--71\%, 47--49\%) with three seeds.
After MX quantisation at $K=32$ the 6- and 8-bit formats are within about one point of each
other on every network, and within $0.2$ points of FP32 on the ViT, so formats can be compared at
matched accuracy.

\paragraph{Campaigns.} Each fault is scored on a fixed, class-balanced set of 200 test images.
Most campaigns draw 3000 faults uniformly over all stored bits. Two \emph{exhaustive} campaigns
flip every one of the $638{,}624$ (e4m3) and $483{,}904$ (e3m2) weight bits of a ResNet8; a
3000-fault estimate lands within 2.4\% of the exhaustive rate and its interval covers it. Every
campaign is tied to its checkpoint by replaying recorded faults. In total the study ran over three
million injections on more than sixty trained networks, on a CPU and two NVIDIA A100 GPUs.

\section{Results}

\subsection{O2: Equal accuracy, very different reliability}

Within about a point of accuracy, element-fault damage spans more than two orders of magnitude on
ResNet8 and nearly four on RepVGG and the ViT (Figure~\ref{fig:accvuln}). On the ViT, e5m2 and
e3m2 differ by $0.01$ points of accuracy, yet an e5m2 element fault is about $2000\times$ as
likely to corrupt an inference. The ordering e3m2 $<$ e4m3 $<$ e5m2 holds on all eight CIFAR-10
configurations with disjoint intervals (Table~\ref{tab:formats-result}) and on all nine
CIFAR-100 networks.

\subsection{O1: Shared-scale faults versus element faults}

A scale fault is more damaging than an element fault on every network and format, from
$1.7\times$ for e5m2, whose element faults are already NaN-dominated, to over $3000\times$ for
e3m2 on the larger networks (Figure~\ref{fig:sites}). In the exhaustive campaigns the scales are
$4.1\%$ of e3m2 storage but carry $87.6\%$ of all damage, and $3.1\%$ of e4m3 storage carrying
$32.7\%$. About 12\% of scale faults give a non-finite output in every format, since E8M0 has its
own NaN code. Flips that grow a block are the dangerous ones: on ResNet8, scale bit 3
(multiplying the block by 256) corrupts 75\% of inferences and bit 7 (overflow) 98\%, while bits
4--6, which shrink the block, corrupt 3\%.

mean over seeds.}

\end{figure}

\subsection{O3: Block size}

Larger blocks shrink the scale's share of storage from $11.1\%$ ($K=8$) to $1.7\%$ ($K=64$) but
make each scale fault worse, while element faults become milder (Figure~\ref{fig:blocksize}). The
element-side trend appears under both the OCP and a non-clipping scale rule, and accuracy moves by
under a point across $K$. The two sites move in opposite directions, so block size is a genuine
trade-off; with protected scales, large blocks are safe.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering

damage per element fault.}
\label{fig:blocksize}
\end{figure}

\subsection{O4: Bit position, layer and tensor type}

Among faults that leave the output finite, the sign bit is the most damaging element bit; in total
damage it ranks seventh of eight in e4m3 (Figure~\ref{fig:perbit}). The $1.27\%$ of element faults
that flip a code into the NaN pattern \texttt{S.1111.111} corrupt every inference and carry
$77.6\%$ of all element damage, and a sign flip is the one flip that cannot reach it. Vulnerability
runs opposite to layer size: the 3584-bit stem convolution is the most fragile layer, the
$304{,}128$-bit final convolution the most robust. Per fault, weights and activations are about
equally dangerous ($0.3$--$2.2\times$ across six networks and two formats), because about 1\% of
faults in either land on a special code; they differ only in exposure, a weight fault persisting
across inferences.

\subsection{O5: What drives vulnerability}

\paragraph{NaN and infinity codes.} A larger network absorbs ordinary perturbations but never a
NaN, and only formats with special codes can produce one. Across the ResNet8 width sweep, a
$35\times$ range of parameters, capacity improves e3m2 (no special codes) $25\times$, e4m3 (two)
$1.5\times$ and e5m2 (eight) not measurably (Figure~\ref{fig:capacity}); the fraction of faults
that produce NaN/Inf stays flat with width. On RepVGG and the ViT, special-code faults carry
97--100\% of e4m3 and e5m2 element damage.

\paragraph{Margin-normalised sensitivity.} For the remaining perturbation damage, define the
sensitivity of a weight as $s_i=|\partial m/\partial w_i|\cdot\mathrm{rms}/m$, where $m$ is the
top-1/top-2 logit margin. It predicts a format-agnostic weight-perturbation probe at $r=+0.998$,
and perturbation damage across all five formats at $r=+0.93$, while mantissa width does not
($r=-0.25$; Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity}). The gradient factor agrees across formats to
$1.08\times$; the spread comes from the margin of the evaluation image nearest a decision
boundary.

\subsection{A read-side guard for special codes}

Decoding any special element code as zero on read turns a NaN-producing fault into a single
zeroed weight. Measured on every architecture with e4m3 and e5m2 (Table~\ref{tab:guard}), the
guard cuts element damage by at least $29\times$ and up to $4100\times$ on RepVGG and the ViT,
bringing e5m2 to the level of e3m2, and by $4$--$53\times$ on ResNet8, which is also fragile to
ordinary perturbations. No guarded fault produced a non-finite output. In hardware it is one
comparator on the read path.

\subsection{CIFAR-100}

CIFAR-100 stands in for ImageNet. Every result above replicates. With smaller margins, the
NaN-free formats become $3$--$22\times$ more vulnerable while e4m3 and e5m2 barely move
(Figure~\ref{fig:c100}), so the gap narrows (on the ViT, e4m3/e3m2 falls from $461\times$ to
$19\times$). The guard still gives $13$--$1387\times$ on RepVGG and the ViT.

\section{Methodological findings}

\paragraph{The any-image SDC metric measures the evaluation set.}
\label{sec:metric}
One fixed fault list replayed against nine disjoint 200-image sets on two networks makes e3m2 and
e4m3 look equal under the any-image metric ($1.03\times$, $0.93\times$), with their order
reversing between sets; per inference they differ $5.6\times$ and $7.6\times$, stable to within
$1.3\times$ (Figure~\ref{fig:evalsets}). The any-image rate saturates and mostly reports whether
the set contains a near-tie input (Spearman $+0.89$ with the near-tie count over 39 networks).
Separately, networks differing only in their training seed differ by a median $12.8\times$ the
injection uncertainty, so results must be replicated over trained networks.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/fig13_eval_sets.pdf}
\caption{One fault list per format replayed on nine evaluation sets, two networks: any-image rate
(top) against per-inference rate (bottom).}
\label{fig:evalsets}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Severity.} The log-ratio severity $|\log v'-\log v|$ scores a sign flip as zero and
ranks failures below chance (AUC $0.435$). The margin-based
$\mathrm{margin\_shift}_i=s_i|v'_i-v_i|/\mathrm{rms}$ reaches AUC $0.990$; flagging
$\mathrm{margin\_shift}>1$ catches $98.5\%$ of failures in $4.4\%$ of the fault space.

\paragraph{Value-aware sampling.} The per-inference target is zero-inflated (92\% of e3m2 element
faults corrupt nothing). Stratifying at quantiles of $\mathrm{margin\_shift}$ with Neyman
allocation~\cite{neyman1934} cuts variance $57$--$120\times$ against uniform sampling, replayed
against exhaustive ground truth. Exact enumeration of the decoded change alone gives
$48$--$65\times$, while TreeFI-style intervals learned from a pilot never beat uniform
($\le1\times$): a pilot cannot see the tail, and in e4m3 the tail is a discrete set of NaN
transitions rather than a value range.

\paragraph{Single versus multi-bit.} Injecting every single and double flip of 2000 words per
format on two networks ($390{,}000$ injections), a double flip does $1.04$--$1.55\times$ the damage
of a single flip and an adjacent pair $0.85$--$1.13\times$. Within-word doubles reach 1\% of
expected damage only at a per-bit flip probability of $2$--$3\times10^{-3}$, far beyond where the
one-fault-per-inference assumption holds. Single-bit injection is sufficient.

\section{Summary and design guidance}

\toprule
objective & answer \\
\midrule
O1 Fault propagation & Scale faults are always more damaging, typically $13\times$ to over

O2 Format sensitivity & Equal-accuracy formats differ by up to three orders of magnitude;
e3m2 $<$ e4m3 $<$ e5m2 on all 17 networks. \\
O3 Block size & Larger blocks make scale faults rarer but worse and element faults milder. \\
O4 Tensor \& location & Weights and activations equally dangerous per fault; NaN transitions

O5 Drivers & NaN/Inf codes first (97--100\% of element damage on larger networks); then the

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\noindent For MX accelerator designers:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Protect the shared scale first}: 2--11\% of storage, $13\times$ to over
$1000\times$ the damage; protect first the scale bits that are normally 0.
\item \textbf{Neutralise special codes}: prefer NaN-free element formats, or decode special codes
as zero on read.
\item \textbf{Do not judge reliability by accuracy or bit width.}
\item \textbf{Treat block size as a trade-off}, and protect by vulnerability (early layers), not
by storage size.
\end{enumerate}
For fault-injection campaigns: report per-inference rates, replicate over trained networks,
inject single-bit faults, and stratify by exact per-code enumeration weighted by sensitivity.

\paragraph{Limitations.} ImageNet was not available on the compute used, so a ViT trained from
scratch on CIFAR-100 stands in for DeiT on ImageNet; the directions of the effects transfer more
reliably than their magnitudes. The largest network has 7M parameters, only faults in stored
values are modelled, and the guard is evaluated in software.

\section{Conclusion}

Reliability of MX networks varies by up to three orders of magnitude across formats of equal
accuracy, and shared-scale faults are always more damaging than element faults. Most element
damage comes from the few flips that land on NaN or infinity codes, which capacity cannot absorb
and a read-side guard removes; the rest follows the network's decision margins. The work also
provides a validated MX fault-injection library, a per-inference metric that avoids the near-tie
confound, and a sampling method that cuts campaign cost by up to two orders of magnitude.

raw results are at \url{https://github.com/peeyushagarwal2004/CS496_UGP}.
